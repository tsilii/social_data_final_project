Section 1

Where You Start Is Everything

Every New York City taxi trip begins with a decision: where to position yourself to find the next fare. That decision — which street, which neighbourhood, which borough — shapes a driver's income more than almost anything else. But how much does it actually matter?

To answer that, we analysed all 29 million credit-card yellow taxi trips made in New York City in 2023, linked every pickup zone to its average tip percentage, and then cross-referenced with US Census Bureau income data (American Community Survey 2023) covering 2,192 census tracts across all five boroughs. What emerged is a portrait of extraordinary inequality: the gap between the highest-tipping and lowest-tipping zones spans more than 26 percentage points.

"A driver whose shift keeps them in the Upper East Side earns tips nearly 300 times larger — as a percentage of fare — than a driver spending the same hours in Arden Heights or Co-Op City."

The explanation turns out to be straightforward: neighbourhood income predicts taxi tip rates better than almost any other factor we tested. Zones where the median household income exceeds $150,000 produce average tips of 23%. Zones where the median income sits below $50,000 average just 6%. The tip map is, in essence, a map of wealth.

29.1M Credit-card taxi trips
2,192 Census tracts with income data
233 Zones with income + tip data
r = 0.57 Spearman: income ↔ tips

We kept only credit-card trips because cash tips are not captured in the data. Tip percentage was calculated as tip ÷ fare × 100, and trips were filtered to remove invalid records (zero-distance, extreme outliers, records outside 2023). The 29.1 million trips that remain represent the reliable, measurable slice of NYC taxi activity.

Neighbourhood income was obtained from the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates for 2023, table B19013 (Median Household Income). Census tract income was spatially joined to taxi zones using area-weighted interpolation.

Section 2

The Tip Map: A City Divided

The map below shows the average tip percentage for each NYC taxi zone, based on pickup location. The pattern is immediately striking: Manhattan concentrates all the deep blue (high-tip) zones, while the outer boroughs — particularly the Bronx and Staten Island — are dominated by orange and red.

Figure 1 — Average tip % by NYC taxi pickup zone (2023 · credit-card trips only). Colour scale: red = lowest tips, blue = highest tips. Hover for zone details.

The geographical clustering is unmistakable. Manhattan's Upper East Side, Upper West Side, and Midtown zones form a dense pocket of high-tip activity. Cross the Harlem River into the Bronx, or head to the outer reaches of Staten Island, and the colour shifts dramatically toward red.

Notably, Queens shows a more mixed picture than Brooklyn or the Bronx. The airport zones (JFK, LaGuardia) and several inner-Queens neighbourhoods reach moderate tip rates — consistent with the mix of airport passengers (who tip generously) and local residents (who tend to tip less).

Section 3

The Highest and Lowest Earning Zones

Zooming to the zone level, the contrast is even more vivid. Upper West Side South leads all pickup zones with a 26.6% average tip across 838,000 trips. The top 10 list reads like a map of Manhattan's most affluent residential corridors: Upper East Side, Lincoln Square, Flatiron, Greenwich Village — all zones where median household incomes exceed $130,000 per year.

At the other extreme, Arden Heights on Staten Island records a 0.09% average tip — essentially zero — across 287 trips. Co-Op City in the Bronx, Far Rockaway in Queens, and Hammels/Arverne round out the bottom, all with average tips below 0.3%.

Section 4

The Income Link: Data Confirms the Pattern

The visual pattern is compelling — but does it hold up statistically? To test this rigorously, we brought in the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey 2023, which provides median household income for every census tract in New York City. We spatially joined those 2,192 tracts to the 263 taxi zones using area-weighted interpolation, giving each zone a precise income estimate drawn from the Census data.

The result: neighbourhood income is strongly correlated with taxi tip rates. The Spearman correlation between median household income and average tip percentage, across 233 zones with sufficient data, is r = 0.574 — statistically highly significant (p < 10⁻²¹). The Pearson correlation is even stronger at r = 0.615.

The income map below mirrors the tip map almost perfectly — deep blue in upper Manhattan, orange and red in the outer boroughs.

Section 5

Crime as a Confirming Signal

We also incorporated 552,429 NYPD crime records from 2023, spatially joined to taxi zones, to see whether crime patterns add anything beyond what income already explains.

We built a weighted danger score per zone and normalised it by zone area in km² — because NYC zones vary enormously in size (0.3 km² in Midtown to 19 km² in Staten Island), and without this normalisation, large zones accumulate crime counts simply by being bigger.

Danger Density = (3 × Felonies + 2 × Misdemeanors + 1 × Violations) ÷ Area (km²)

Once normalised, the crime-density correlation with tips is positive (r = +0.335) — the same direction as income. This makes sense: the most crime-dense zones are Manhattan's tiny commercial blocks (Penn Station, Times Square, Garment District), which are also the highest-income, highest-tip zones. Crime density here is a proxy for urban commercial intensity, not residential danger. Both variables — income and crime density — are pointing at the same underlying reality.

Section 6

The Night Effect

The income-tipping relationship holds consistently across both day and night. Repeating the crime-density correlation analysis separately for daytime and nighttime trips gives r = +0.338 (day) and r = +0.323 (night) — nearly identical. The structural character of each zone — wealthy and commercial vs lower-income and residential — determines tipping behaviour regardless of the hour.

Within that stable structure, an additional pattern emerges from the hourly data: tip percentages in Manhattan show a modest evening bump during leisure hours (restaurants, bars, entertainment venues), while late-night outer-borough trips — residents returning home — drag the city-wide average slightly downward after midnight.

Time of day modulates the absolute values but does not close the structural gap between zones.

Section 7

What We Can and Cannot Conclude

  • Correlation, not causation. A high neighbourhood income does not directly cause passengers to tip more. Income is associated with trip type, passenger profile, and payment habits — all of which influence tipping together.
  • Cash tips are invisible. We use only credit-card trips. Cash tips — potentially more common in outer-borough, lower-income areas — are entirely missing. This could narrow the true gap, because outer-borough passengers who tip in cash appear as zero-tip in our data.
  • Yellow taxis are not representative of all mobility. Yellow cabs operate primarily in Manhattan and at airports. Outer boroughs increasingly rely on Uber, Lyft, and green cabs, so our data disproportionately represents certain trip types and passenger demographics.
  • ACS income is an area average, not the passenger's income. We assign zone income based on census tracts — but a taxi passenger in a high-income zone may not themselves be high-income. Zone-level analysis risks the ecological fallacy when applied to individuals.
  • Crime records reflect reporting rates. The NYPD data captures reported crime, which varies by community trust, police presence, and reporting culture.
  • Low outer-borough trip volumes. Some outer-borough zones have very few yellow taxi trips (dozens, not thousands), making their average tip rates volatile. We filtered to zones with at least 50 trips for correlations and 200–500 for rankings.

These caveats are important — but they do not undermine the main finding. The borough-level averages (Manhattan 25.2%, Bronx 1.5%, Staten Island 0.5%) are based on millions of trips and are not sensitive to small-sample noise. The income–tip relationship (r = 0.574) is robust and consistent across multiple analytical approaches. The economic geography of NYC taxi tipping is a large-scale, durable pattern.

Section 8

Conclusion: The City's Invisible Fare

New York City's taxi tip geography is a map of economic inequality drawn one transaction at a time. The data is unambiguous: drivers who work the Upper East Side, Upper West Side, and Midtown Manhattan corridors earn tips averaging 25–27% per fare. Drivers whose shifts keep them in the Bronx, outer Queens, or Staten Island earn a fraction of that — often less than 1% of fare.

The US Census income data explains why. With a Spearman correlation of r = 0.574, neighbourhood median household income is by far the strongest predictor of taxi tip rates we found — stronger than crime density, stronger than borough classification, consistent across day and night. The near-fourfold jump from the lowest-income quintile (6.4% tip) to the highest (23.3%) is not a quirk of data — it is a structural feature of how wealth distributes across the city and how that distribution shapes everyday economic behaviour.

"In New York City, the taxi meter starts at the same rate in every neighbourhood. But the tip — that small, voluntary act of generosity — traces the city's economic divide with remarkable precision."

The NYPD crime data, once normalised by zone area, confirms the same picture from a different angle: the most crime-dense zones are Manhattan's tiny commercial corridors, which are also the wealthiest and highest-tipping zones. Crime density and tip rates are both expressions of urban commercial intensity — and both, ultimately, of the concentration of wealth in a narrow strip of Manhattan.

For taxi drivers, this analysis underscores a reality they already know: where you work in New York is not neutral. The invisible fare — the tip — is not equally distributed across the five boroughs. It follows income, it follows density, and it follows the economic geography of a city that concentrates opportunity and wealth in ways that are visible, measurable, and persistent.
